The Era of Algorithmic Health in Portugal

Regulation, Engineering, and Strategy for HealthTech in 2026

SG
Steve Green
January 30, 2026
15 min read

The narrative that Artificial Intelligence will revolutionize medicine is well-known. However, in 2026, those operating on the ground in Portugal know that the reality has shifted. The viability of a HealthTech project no longer depends solely on computational capacity — it depends on the ability to navigate an increasingly demanding regulatory environment.

This article proposes a structured analysis: what the law requires, why implementation is difficult, what technical solutions are available, and where those solutions fall short.

I. The New Regulatory Framework

The official vision for 2025-2026 is unequivocal: AI is welcome in healthcare, provided it is rigorously regulated ex-ante. We no longer operate solely under GDPR — we operate under a legislative corpus anchored in the AI Act, which classifies most medical AI applications as High-Risk technology.

SPMS (Shared Services of the Ministry of Health) has established doctrine through recent White Papers:

Key Requirements
  • Mandatory compliance with ISO/IEC 42001 and the NIS2 directive for integration into the national health ecosystem.
  • Mandatory interoperability via HL7 FHIR — it is no longer optional; it is a fundamental requirement for semantic data sharing.
  • Data sovereignty under the European Health Data Space (EHDS), which imposes strict rules on the primary and secondary use of clinical information.

II. Structural Roadblocks

When trying to apply this regulatory framework to operational reality, significant obstacles arise. Official literature recognizes three; field experience reveals more.

The Data Paradox

Regulation demands high-quality, representative, and unbiased datasets. However, obtaining real clinical data that is structured and properly consented is extraordinarily difficult due to system fragmentation and data protection requirements. This creates a vicious cycle: to prove the model works, data is needed; to get data, one must prove the model is safe.

The Clinical Responsibility Issue

If AI operates as a "black box," the problem academic literature calls the epistemic cost of opacity arises. When a doctor cannot explain the reasoning behind an algorithmic recommendation, their position becomes vulnerable. The Medical Association (Ordem dos Médicos) reinforces that telemedicine and AI should be decision-support tools, not replacement mechanisms.

Communication Restrictions

The ERS (Health Regulatory Authority) Manual of Good Practices in Health Advertising strictly prohibits the use of superlatives ("the best," "pioneer") or guarantees of results without robust scientific evidence. This significantly limits traditional go-to-market strategies.

Institutional Inertia

Less documented but equally relevant: the SNS (National Health Service) operates with procurement cycles of 18-24 months, legacy systems that predate the digital era, and decision-maker turnover that makes any commercial relationship precarious. Even with total regulatory compliance, a startup might exhaust its runway before closing a sale.

Shortage of Specialized Talent

Portugal has a limited number of professionals who combine expertise in machine learning, clinical terminologies (SNOMED, ICD-11), regulatory requirements (MDR, AI Act), and the ability to communicate with medical stakeholders. Informal estimates point to 50-100 professionals with this complete profile.

Lack of Reimbursement Model

Even if the product exists, is compliant, and is clinically validated, the question remains: who pays? The SNS does not have a reimbursement table for "clinical decision support AI." Without a clear economic framework, institutional adoption remains slow.

III. Technical Solutions

Engineering has developed approaches for some of these problems. Success depends on a strategy that combines technical compliance with institutional viability.

Federated Learning

This architecture allows collaboration between institutions without compromising privacy. Instead of centralizing data in a server, the algorithm moves to the local data. Each hospital trains the model with its own data; only the mathematical parameters (weights) are shared and aggregated centrally.

European projects like DataTools4Heart (cardiology) and frameworks like FED-EHR demonstrate that it is possible to create robust models without centralizing sensitive data.

def federated_averaging(local_updates):
    """
    Simplified federated aggregation.
    The central server receives only mathematical weights,
    never patient data.
    """
    aggregated = []
    for weights in zip(*local_updates):
        aggregated.append(sum(weights) / len(weights))
    return aggregated

Synthetic Data

The generation of artificial data is validated by the European Commission through the Virtual Human Twins initiative as a path for development and testing. If the generation process is statistically irreversible, it significantly mitigates privacy risks.

Available tools include Synthea (open-source), CTGAN, Gretel.ai, and Mostly AI.

B2B Strategy

To bypass ERS communication restrictions, successful players focus on B2B sales and clinical efficiency metrics. Companies like Glintt position themselves as technological partners; UpHill focuses on automating care journeys; Knok leads in teleconsultation integration.

Regulatory compliance becomes a selling point: a Clinical Director buys safety, not just functionality.

IV. Limitations of the Solutions

It is important to recognize that the proposed technical solutions have their own limitations.

Federated Learning Limitations

Data Heterogeneity: The aggregation algorithm assumes similar statistical distributions across institutions. In the Portuguese reality, epidemiological profiles vary significantly between a central hospital in Lisbon and a district hospital in the interior. Simple aggregation can degrade the model's global performance.

Non-absolute Privacy: Attacks documented in literature (membership inference, gradient leakage) show that weight sharing can, under certain conditions, reveal information about the original data. Mitigations like Differential Privacy exist but introduce performance trade-offs.

Institutional Complexity: Implementing FL across multiple SNS hospitals requires approval from various Ethics Committees, bilateral legal agreements, local infrastructure installation, and governance definition. Realistic estimate: 18-36 months for a functional pilot.

Synthetic Data Limitations

Clinical Validation: Synthetic data is useful for development, but the regulator will demand evidence of efficacy in real patients. It does not replace the need for access to clinical data for final validation.

Perpetuation of Bias: Generative models learn — and can amplify — the biases present in the original data. A predominantly urban dataset will generate predominantly urban synthetic data.

Unstable Regulatory Framework: The EHDS mentions synthetic data as a promising path, but technical criteria for "irreversible anonymization" are not yet consolidated.

B2B Limitations in the SNS

Prolonged Sales Cycles: From the first contact to the first euro of revenue, the typical cycle in the SNS is 27-60 months.

Political Risk: Changes in government or hospital management can restart procurement processes.

V. Alternative Strategies

Validation in the Private Sector

The article focused on the SNS as it is the largest market. But for an early-stage startup, the private sector offers advantages: groups like CUF or Luz Saúde have decision-making autonomy and shorter purchase cycles. Insurers like Médis or Multicare have clear incentives to adopt solutions that reduce costs. Validating in the private sector and scaling to the public with accumulated evidence can be a more sustainable trajectory.

Focus on Interoperability

Synthetic data generation is becoming a commodity (Synthea is free). A more defensible differentiator may be solving the interoperability problem — many Portuguese hospital systems still operate on HL7 v2 or proprietary formats. Those who facilitate the transition to HL7 FHIR will have a significant moat.

Regulatory Sandbox Proposal

Portugal has experience with regulatory sandboxes (Bank of Portugal for fintech). A structured proposal for an equivalent regime in HealthTech — with joint supervision from CNPD, ERS, and SPMS, defined scale limits, and a path to full compliance — could accelerate innovation without compromising user protection.

VI. Viability Checklist

Before moving forward with an AI project in healthcare, consider:

Technical Dimension

  • Does the solution support HL7 FHIR?
  • Is there a strategy to handle data heterogeneity in a federated context?
  • Do synthetic data have a clinical validation plan?
  • Does the model offer minimum explainability (SHAP, attention maps, or equivalent)?

Regulatory Dimension

  • Is the risk classification according to the AI Act documented?
  • Has a DPIA (Data Protection Impact Assessment) been performed?
  • Is the path to CE marking defined?
  • Is the communication material compliant with ERS guidelines?

Institutional Dimension

  • Is there a confirmed hospital partner for a pilot?
  • Has the relevant Ethics Committee been contacted?
  • Is an internal sponsor identified in the target institution?

Business Dimension

  • Is the reimbursement model validated?
  • Is the pipeline diversified (not exclusively dependent on the SNS)?
  • Is the runway sufficient for a 24-36 month sales cycle?
  • Does the team combine technical, clinical, and regulatory expertise?

VII. Conclusion

The "Far West" period of digital health is over. The era of Regulated Algorithmic Health requires simultaneous competence across multiple dimensions: technology, regulation, business model, institutional relations, and — no less importantly — patience.

The startups that will prosper are not necessarily the most technically advanced. They will be the ones that best navigate the ecosystem's complexity, turning regulatory compliance into a competitive advantage and building lasting institutional relationships.

Regulation is not just an obstacle — it is also a filter that eliminates less prepared competitors and a barrier to entry that protects those who overcome it first.
